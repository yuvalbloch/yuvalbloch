## how to change my mind 
Three days ago, I sat in a crowded bar in Be'er Sheva, listening to a neuroscience lecture. "Science at the Bar" has become a common trend in Israel, but for me, it was a first. The topic was trendy—psychedelics and their implications for psychiatric therapy—but the takeaway hit on something much deeper: **the physics of personal change.**

The lecturer described the brain through the lens of **entropy**—a measure of disorder. Life exists on a delicate balance: too much order, and we lose the ability to adapt; too much chaos, and we fall apart.

She explained that a "low entropy" state is dominated by prediction. We know who we are, and we know how the world works. In this state, we stop truly "sensing" because our brains simply autocomplete the reality we expect to see. It is a rigid, top-down hierarchy.

"High entropy," conversely, is an exploratory state. We let go of what we know, allowing our brain activity to spread across different processing systems. It is chaotic, yes, but it is the only state where true change can happen.

For the last two months, I have been trying to force myself to change—to be more focused, more aware, and less dependent on the digital world. My instinct was to do this "top-down": make a decision and force my mind to follow. But sitting in that bar, I realized I might have it backward.

**True change doesn't come from holding on; it comes from releasing.** This will be the theme of this post.

### The Nature of Freedom

One section of my personal plan was to define specific hours for "distraction behavior." For example, I decided I would only use Facebook between 19:00 and 21:00.

In the beginning, I expected this to be incredibly hard; I thought I would spend those two hours binging. Surprisingly, very fast into the plan, I stopped using it almost entirely. I didn't even want to look at Facebook during the times I had set aside for it.

Looking back, I understand now that I used to do many things I didn't actually enjoy. I had just become so used to doing them that whenever I reached for my phone, I never stopped to ask if I _wanted_ to do it.

While my plan was based on strict rules, I wasn't always able to follow them perfectly. I actually broke them many times. But the rules served a different purpose: they gave me the opportunity to observe my behavior. The rules created a pause—a space to put my attention on the question: **"What do I really want?"**

The rules didn't just control me; they shook my routine enough to make me unsure, breaking the automatic cycle.
