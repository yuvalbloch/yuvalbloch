# How to Change your Mind

Three days ago, I sat in a crowded bar in Be'er Sheva, listening to a neuroscience lecture. "Science at the Bar" has become a popular trend in Israel, but for me, it was a first. The topic was trendy—psychedelics and their implications for psychiatric therapy—but the takeaway hit on something much deeper: **the physics of personal change.**

The lecturer described the brain through the lens of **entropy**—a measure of disorder. Life exists on a delicate balance: too much order, and we lose the ability to adapt; too much chaos, and we fall apart.

She explained that a **"low entropy"** state is dominated by prediction. We know who we are, and we know how the world works. In this state, we stop truly "sensing" because our brains simply autocomplete the reality we expect to see. It is a rigid, top-down hierarchy.

**"High entropy,"** conversely, is an exploratory state. We let go of what we know, allowing brain activity to spread across different processing systems. It is chaotic, yes, but it is the only state where true change can happen.

For the last two months, I have been trying to force myself to change—to be more focused, more aware, and less dependent on the digital world. My instinct was to do this "top-down": make a decision and force my mind to follow. But sitting in that bar, I realized I had it backward.

**True change doesn't come from holding on; it comes from releasing.**



### The Nature of Freedom

One part of my personal growth plan was to define specific hours for "distraction behavior." I decided I would only use Facebook between 19:00 and 21:00.

In the beginning, I expected this to be incredibly difficult; I thought I would spend those two hours binging. Surprisingly, I stopped using it almost entirely. I didn’t even _want_ to look at Facebook during the times I had set aside for it.

I realized I had been doing many things I didn't actually enjoy. I had simply become so used to doing them that whenever I reached for my phone, I never stopped to ask if I actually wanted to. While my plan was based on strict rules, I didn't always follow them perfectly. I broke them many times. But the rules served a purpose beyond restriction: they created a **pause.** They gave me a space to put my attention on the question: _"What do I really want?"_

The rules didn't just control me; they shook my routine enough to break the automatic cycle.

### External Tools and the "Moment of Pause"

Realizing the need for that pause highlighted why I need external tools. I use an app called **Freedom**, though there are many like it. It allows you to block apps for set focus sessions. I use relatively "soft" settings, meaning I can bypass or stop the sessions at any time.

Because I could bypass the app, I initially believed it was a minor, optional addition to my plan. I thought I could do it without the help. I was wrong. When the app stopped working due to a technical glitch with the Android system, my plan fell apart. I discovered that while all I needed was a tiny pause to think before opening Facebook, I truly needed something external to _force_ that moment of hesitation.



### Lessons from Climbing

Using the time I saved by removing habits I didn't like, I finally took up a hobby I’d wanted to try for a while: **bouldering.** I soon found that climbing is a microcosm of my life and career.

Like academic work, climbing requires remarkable focus. It requires you to be comfortable "hanging in the air," unsure if you are taking the right step. Sometimes you must take a **leap of faith**—leaving both hands at once, trusting you will catch the next hold. You must allow yourself to fall repeatedly.

But bouldering teaches one lesson more effectively than the university: **letting go of the goal.** In climbing, you plan your route (the "beta")—which hand goes where, how to set your leg, whether to use a Drop-Knee or a rock-over. But once you are on the wall, you must forget the goal. You need all your attention for the **next move.** If you focus on the top, you lose the movement. By "forgetting" the finish line, you release your body to do what it already knows how to do. Often, when I successfully solve a route I’ve struggled with, I don’t even feel a rush of excitement—I had forgotten there was a goal at all.

This applies to my academic work as well. My PI (Principal Investigator) taught me that it is important to make plans and schedule the steps toward publishing a paper. But only now do I understand the real value of the plan: **When I have a plan, I am free to forget the goal and simply follow the steps.**



## The Fluid Art of Becoming

Personal change complicates this logic. Some people define a clear vision of who they want to become and work toward it. For me, this approach makes little sense. What I want depends on who I am—and who I am changes as I act. Identity is a complex system with feedback loops (I explore this further [here](https://yuvalbloch.com/blog/identity/)).

This suggests going one step further: releasing goals entirely and committing only to the _next step_. In each moment, asking what action feels aligned—without a fixed destination.

This is also the nature of academic research. While I know the general topic of my work, early results constantly reshape its direction. I am walking an uncharted landscape, discovering value rather than executing a blueprint.

From this process, I adopted a mantra for this period of my life:

**“Everything I do, I do for the art of doing it.”**



## Stabilizing a New Mental State

“Entropy is the cost of structure.”

Building something new requires releasing old beliefs—but those beliefs once served a purpose. Knowing who you are can be protective, especially on the unstable ground of the Middle East.

Lately, I feel myself slowly settling into a new equilibrium. I have released habits that no longer serve me (like constant social media checking). I have adopted new ones that do (like climbing). Some habits are still missing (such as summarizing each day in the evening), and some questions remain open—especially the problem of the news (see below).

For now, I am stabilizing, reducing again the entrohy in my system, there will be more change to be done, but there will be also right time to do it.

### The Red Queen’s Information Trap

I am not the only thing changing; the world is changing, too. The velocity of information has increased, specifically within the news cycle. As a citizen in a democracy, I consider it my duty to stay informed, yet the modern news cycle has become a primary engine of distraction.

I tried limiting myself to checking the news only twice a day, but I ran into a paradox: when headlines rotate every hour, there is no guarantee that the "important" news will still be visible when I finally look. This constant churn doesn't provide more clarity; it creates an unorganized overflow of irrelevant detail that makes it harder to grasp what is actually happening.

This isn't limited to the news. Many of our primary tools for learning have been redesigned to include "distraction traps." A friend recently told me she used to rely on YouTube for her studies, but since the introduction of "Shorts," it has become nearly impossible to focus on the platform without getting pulled into a loop of mindless scrolling.

This suggests that reaching a "better state" is far from a static achievement. To borrow from evolutionary biology, we are living out the **Red Queen Hypothesis**: we must keep running just to stay in the same place. Stabilizing my mind is not a one-time fix; it is a dynamic process of constant adaptation as the tools around me continue to shift. For now, I am focusing on slowing down—but I know I can never truly stop moving.
