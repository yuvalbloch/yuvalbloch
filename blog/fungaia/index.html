Uncharted Life

yuval bloch

To Mayana

This story is about intelligence. There is more than one way to be intelligent, but you already know that I am intelligent in a different way than you, or any other friend of ours. In fact, I believe that no two people on Earth are intelligent in the same way. Yet, there is one thing that is similar to all of us: we all think in a sequential way—we know how to put one idea in front of another.

In this story, I propose an idea: what if it doesn’t have to be like that? What if on some other planet, a member of the Fungi family and not the Animal family was the first to build a civilization? In that case, the parallel mycelium and not the hierarchical neural network would become the main way of thinking, it a strange idea. Still, I think if any of us can understand what it means to live in parallel, it’s probably you. so I dedicates this story for you

First Contact

You probably all remember the moment on July 20, 2555, when the famous words, “I think we made a first contact,” sounded on every radio and television on Earth. No matter what you did at the moment, someone probably called you to listen.

Less people are aware that this message was actually edited for the benefit of the conservative crowd (and the honor of the International Space Agency). The full sentence sent in the radio wave seven months before was, “Fuck, I think we made a first contact.” While edited by the agency, the “fuck” at the beginning of the sentence perfectly described the feeling of Captain Elinor when she first saw the Fungaian star system. This is definitely not how she imagined first contact.

Captain’s Log: January 20, 2555, 13:00 Greenwich Clock

Today, we are finally going to finish circling Beta 8 and start approaching the Beta 7 star system. It is an important objective in our journey as it has a planet that might be capable of supporting life. Unfortunately, we don’t have the opportunity yet to have a good lock on it, as the light from Beta 8 is blinding our sensitive equipment. We did identify some gravitational anomalies, so we are all highly alert today, ready to capture every piece of information about the new star system and, in case of need, change flight trajectories. I record this while we are all watching the perfectly white screens, waiting for some detail to appear and feeling as useless as flowerpots.

Wait.

It’s starting.

Oh, shit, what the hell is this?

Oh, shit.

Someone put on the radio channel to Earth.

Do you all see this?

Fuck, I think we made a first contact.

End of recording.

Captain’s Log: January 20, 2555, 17:00 Greenwich Clock

There is a lot to do, but I am making this record for the historians that will come. They will want to look on it if we ever return to Earth, which I currently doubt. As we speak, we are busy destroying every record of Earth located on the ship. We saw a star system inhabited with an intelligent species. They are probably more advanced than us, and we have yet to know if they saw us. We are trying to return to the shade of Beta 8, activating engines on full power, but at our current speed, it will take at least three days, during which we will be totally exposed to any radar in this star system.

Well, let’s start with a description of what we saw.

All planets in the Beta 7 star system are inhabited with plant life, or something similar to plant life. They are colorful, with green being rare. Our astrobiologist, Yuval, is certain that these are photosynthetic elements capturing light. Looking at the surface of each one of those planets, you get a strange feeling. While the configuration of the plant life might appear random at first sight, looking deeper makes you notice perfect fractals appearing, from the entire planetary scale to the pixel size of our cameras.

But this will not be the first thing you notice looking on the star system. No, the first thing you notice is the spiderweb holding the entire system together: a web of thin wires connecting each pair of planets and the tiny lights appearing on it. The wires move together with the planets in a complex dance, enabling them to stay connected without entangling with each other.

Captain’s Log: January 21, 2555, 02:00 Greenwich Clock

Murphy was a genius. Of course, at this very moment when we are trying to move away from a planet with what is clearly a superior life form, our engine will shut down. My first engineer, Carl, estimates it will take two days to activate again, and for now, we are stuck in a circular route around Beta 7, which will bring us into proximity with at least two different planets before we can get off.

Yet for now, it doesn’t look like they notice us. No space probe was launched from any of the planets. This is actually surprising for such an advanced civilization; we don’t see any spacecraft, only those thin wires connecting the planets and those lights that now we know are moving, just slowly—around 100 miles per hour, which from a distance is invisible to the human eye. We also started capturing radio waves, but we don’t manage to get anything from them. It looks like they communicate on so many channels that, for our equipment, it’s impossible to differentiate.

Captain’s Log: January 21, 2555, 20:00 Greenwich Clock

We are getting closer to the sixth planet (in distance from the sun) and do not yet have any proof that they notice us. I start to suspect that it might be an abandoned star system that is keeping the relics from a civilization that no longer exists.

Taking a closer look at the planet, close to the surface, the wires are splitting into thousands of even smaller wires that soon go underground. Besides the strange geometry of the plant life, we see almost no sign of civilization, only a few giant, colorful spheres that shine in infra-red—probably some factories or power plants.

Captain’s Log: January 22, 2555, 02:00 Greenwich Clock

We didn’t notice it in the beginning because it was so thin. Then, when we noticed the tiny wire lay in our flight trajectory, it looked lifeless, and we estimated that we would pass thousands of kilometers from it. It grew so elegantly that it took us too long to notice it was actually growing. Then it started splitting, creating a network like a fisherman’s network. Even without the main engine, we might still be able to go around it using our second engines, but what good will that be? We are stuck in this route, and if they want, they will capture us.

We show no resistance—a sign of peace—and are sending the ready-made radio message at all channels. Still, I open the cyanide box and spread it to the crew. We are ready for the worst.

Captain’s Log: January 22, 2555, 03:00 Greenwich Clock

The net captured us surprisingly softly. We almost felt nothing when we slowed down until our speed aligned with those of the planets. As it slowed down, thousands of tiny wires started writing on our window. Each wrote one letter. The result was a total mess of English letters put with no order. Our linguist says that the distribution of letters looks like English, but the order was totally random.

Captain’s Log: January 22, 2555, 05:00 Greenwich Clock

After two hours of showing them text, it looks like they are starting to understand how it works. Now they put reasonable text, asking around 30 questions, like:

  • How are you?

  • What you are doing here?

  • What are you like to eat?

  • what are the diffrent between I and we

And so on. But the process was weird. Each one of them started with a random point in the sentence, writing slowly a letter (around 10 seconds), then they added more and more letters, like they are solving a crossword, until all questions were complete, almost parallelly.
